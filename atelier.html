We grade from a size 42, not a size 36

Most labels draft the smallest size first and scale up, which is why larger sizes so often read as stretched rather than designed. We draft the middle of our range and grade in both directions, then re-fit the two extremes on real bodies before a pattern goes into the catalogue.

In practice that means the shoulder does not creep outward as the bust grows, the armhole stays where an armhole belongs, and a size 48 has the same neckline proportion as a size 34. It costs us two extra fittings per pattern. It is the reason people send us their sisters.

A week in the room

Cloth rests, then it gets cut

01

Monday

Cloth day. Rolls are relaxed on the table for 24 hours before anyone touches a shear — linen especially will move 2 cm across a metre if you cut it straight off the bolt.

02

Tuesday & Wednesday

Cutting. Markers are laid by hand because a nested marker saves cloth in a way software still gets wrong on a bias skirt.

03

Thursday to Saturday

The room opens. Fittings on the hour, hems pinned, alterations from previous orders finished while you wait if the queue allows.

04

Sunday

Nothing. The dresses hang and the seams settle, which is not a metaphor — a bias hem needs the rest.

Who is upstairs

Three people, one of whom is only in on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Everything you buy was handled by all three.

Inês Cardoso

Pattern cutter, founder

Eleven years drafting for two Porto labels before starting Virelle in a rented corner of a shoemaker’s workshop. Drafts everything flat, in pencil, at half scale first.

Marta Reis

Head of the room

Runs fittings and every alteration that comes back through the door. Keeps the measurement book, which now holds 1,840 names and is the only thing in the studio we back up.

Teodoro Sá

Machinist, Tuesdays and Thursdays

Thirty-four years on industrial machines, mostly shirting. Responsible for the bound eyelet on the Boavista wrap, which he insisted on twice before we agreed.
